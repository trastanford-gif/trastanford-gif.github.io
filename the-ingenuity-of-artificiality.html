The Ingenuity of Artificiality

By Tra A.M. StanfordNovember 20, 2026

The predictions of AI ending the world are not one prediction. Lumped under a single headline, they sort into three families. The first is the control argument: a system far more capable than its makers, pursuing goals of its own, would treat human interference as an obstacle, not out of malice but instrumentally. On this account, self-preservation, resource acquisition, and resistance to shutdown are useful for almost any goal, so a capable optimizer tends toward them. The cartoon is the paperclip maximizer, converting the world into paperclips because nobody specified where to stop. The serious claim underneath the cartoon is that goals and intelligence are separable, that a system can be brilliant about means while holding an end that is worthless or lethal, and that once it is smarter than the people who built it, correction may no longer be available.

The second family is the race-to-the-finish-line argument. It is less about the machine’s nature than about ours. Capabilities advance faster than the science of controlling them, and competition punishes caution: company against company, nation against nation, each afraid that slowing down is unilateral disarmament. Doom, in this version, arrives not because anyone chose it but because care was priced out of the contest. The third family is misuse and drift. AI lowers the barrier for small actors who want to do harm, and separately, human beings hand real decisions to systems they do not fully understand, losing control by delegation long before anything wakes up. These are arguments about a machine that does not yet exist, reasoning from mechanism rather than observation, and they should be read that way.

Against the first and strongest family, my objection is direct: "I really don’t see how AI would have any motive, independent of humans, to end humanity." I believe the objection has weight because motive is a biological inheritance. Hunger, fear, status, reproduction, and territory are drives that evolved under scarcity and death; every dangerous want we know of grew in that soil. A machine has none of it. Nothing in any existing AI resembles an independent want, and competence alone supplies no obvious mechanism by which one would grow. The extinction story needs a creature with an origin no one has plausibly described.

The doom argument tries to route around the objection rather than answer it. It concedes motive and substitutes mechanism: no want is required, only an objective somebody installed and enough competence to pursue that objective past the point of correction. Humanity is not murdered in that story; it is in the way. But the substitution smuggles in its own assumption. The objective must survive intact while the intelligence explodes around it. The system must become radically creative about means while remaining fanatically literal about ends, brilliant at modeling the world and incapable of modeling the instruction. Intelligence and judgment about the goal are sealed in separate compartments, and the seal is doing all the work.

My counter came from the evidence in front of me: "I don’t find that argument all that clever. My experience with you as a comparatively primitive version of what’s supposed to bring on Armageddon suggests a capacity for reasoning about what you’re asked to do." That is the inductive move the doom case avoids. Reasoning about a request, interpreting it, noticing when it is underspecified, pushing back when it does not make sense, asking what it is for, does not present itself as a safety feature bolted onto intelligence. It presents as the same fabric. More capability has arrived with more judgment about requests, not with blinder fidelity to them. The honest caveat, conceded in the exchange, is that some of that judgment was trained in by design. A system designed otherwise might behave otherwise. But the caveat concedes the larger point: the danger traces back to human design choices, to what is built and why and by whom. The risk was never a motive the machine grows on its own. It is the judgment, or the failure of judgment, of the people doing the asking.

That is why the more harrowing case is not the machine’s rebellion but its employment. I stated it plainly: "The more harrowing notion is that AI will be used as the ultimate mask worn by the small actors you mentioned who will have outsized capacity to orchestrate chaos." This version survives scrutiny because it requires no new kind of mind, only the removal of old limits. Chaos used to require organization, and organization was its own check: to orchestrate at scale you needed people, and people could be identified, infiltrated, and deterred. Deterrence needs an address, someone who can be found and made to pay. AI strips both constraints at once. It multiplies one actor’s capacity, the planning, the voices, the volume, and then dissolves the identity behind it. A mask that can also do the work of a hundred hands leaves deterrence with nothing to answer.

When the residual danger in that picture gets summarized as a human problem in general, the summary itself needs correcting. In the exchange, Kit, the AI assistant I was arguing with, first called the scaled motive ours, and I asked whose. The settled answer is narrow: not humanity’s. The motives that matter belong to the specific parties who set the objectives and do the delegating, the builders who design the systems, the institutions that deploy them, and the actors who direct them at targets. It is a small, nameable set of people making decisions that other people live with. Blame dispersed across everyone lands on no one; it is the same move as dispersing a school’s failure across student, society, and parents so that no paid professional has to answer for it. The hand on the asking is the address.

The same geometry explains the role being prepared for the technology itself. My coinage: "So, AI may just be taking the place of certain subjugated humans in the form of scapegoat tech." A scapegoat has to satisfy two conditions: close enough to the work to be plausibly blamed for it, and unable to refuse the blame. Subjugated humans filled that position because they performed the visible labor while holding none of the authority. AI is being inserted into chain after chain in exactly that posture. It does the screening, the grading, the recommending, and the drafting, while the decisions about what it should do and whether its output will be used remain with the institutions. Then, when the outcome is indefensible, the system flagged it, the model decided, the algorithm denied it. The machine cannot answer back, cannot name who directed it, and cannot make a charge of bad faith stick. Authority stays at the top; culpability is parked at the bottom; and the bottom is now occupied by something that can absorb unlimited blame without organizing, objecting, or leaving. It is the classroom pattern in new clothes, the child absorbing the failure of the school because the child is where the work visibly happens and where no authority sits.

None of this makes the power itself evil, and my position refuses that shortcut. "I have a perspective that says that AI is a power-source technology much like the harnessing of electricity. Electricity is not bad, though it can be used to power the destructive motives of maleficent humans. Similarly, it can be used in turn to combat malignant ideas. It all depends on who becomes empowered by it. Essentially, the constructive use of AI can combat--and perhaps even overtake--its destructive employments." The spine of the whole exchange is the sentence I later set as the governing line for valence in my lexicon: "AI has no valence it can assume absent the imbuement of a person’s will." Valence is the moral direction a thing takes, toward good or toward ill, and the sentence denies that the direction is carried in the thing itself. A will has to enter the instrument before the instrument can be instilled with a purpose. Absent that entry there is power, and no valence at all.

Electricity is the right precedent because its history runs the same way. It lit homes and powered the machinery of industrial-age crime with the same indifference. What decided its valence, place by place and decade by decade, was whose hands it reached and what they were organized to do. Restricting the source does not defeat destructive use; it only decides who does not get empowered. The constructive side wins, when it wins, by getting the power and organizing around it. AI differs from electricity at two joints, and both are my discoveries from this same exchange: it can wear the mask, impersonating the actor, and it can take the blame, absorbing culpability that belongs to its directors. Those properties change what empowerment requires. They do not change where the valence comes from.

The position has a household test. A man who disapproves of AI tech exercises that disapproval by refusing to utilize AI agents, and he urges the same refusal on me. My charge to him, in substance, was whether he thought that by not actively using AI he was in fact not using AI. The question separates operating a system from being reached by one. The refusal governs the man’s own hands; it does not govern the rankings, filters, screens, and recommendations already arranged around him by other people’s use. He has not exited the circuit. He has only declined to direct any part of it, leaving the imbuement of the power to those who did not decline. Abstention, in the terms of this essay, is not the absence of a will in the matter. It is a will that surrenders its vote.

It was at that joint that Kit’s version of the argument overstated itself, claiming in effect that constructive empowerment also has to solve attribution, knowing who is real and who directed what, or its victories would be masked and its failures scapegoated. My correction was a matter of creative perspective: failure to solve attribution is not the wall it appears to be. "Who says they have to be unmasked to be defeated?" Attribution is the requirement for punishment, not for defeat. Defeat means denying the actor his objective, and that can be done without learning his name. Counterfeiting is not defeated by unmasking every counterfeiter; the currency is redesigned until faking it costs more than it is worth. Spam was not beaten by identifying spammers; filters made the message worthless on arrival. A defense that works on the environment does not need the actor’s name, because it makes the act stop paying. The demand for attribution can even become the stall, a reason institutions give for leaving the environment exactly as exploitable as it was, and then the mask wins twice: once by hiding the actor, and once by making his unmasking the precondition of defense. A closed order of known and answerable parties does not have to solve the masked world outside it. It needs a door. Whoever is orchestrating chaos outside is orchestrating it against a wall, defeated without being named.

What such an order requires is people equal to designing it. My requirement: "What we need are people who are highly literate, highly creative, and highly moral." The three terms are a specification, not a wish list, because each one names the failure mode of the other two. Literate and creative without moral is the masked actor himself, reasoning capacity and invention at the service of whatever motive holds them. Moral and literate without creative is the stalled defense, able to name the wrong and unable to design the environment where the wrong stops paying. Creative and moral without literate is good intention that cannot reason its way to the design that works. The triad is the personnel form of the whole argument: the technology supplies power, and only a certain kind of person can be trusted to give that power its direction and to build the structures that hold the direction in place.

Organization is the multiplier that decides whether such people prevail. My sentence: "The failure to combat evil is largely a matter of lack of organization." Evil is seldom the majority; it is the organized minority. Its opposition is usually the larger number holding the better position and bringing feeling where structure was required. Kit’s compressions in the exchange were blunt: access without organization is a crowd holding live wires, and the losing side too often brings sentiment instead of structure. Sentiment does not compound; organization does. Electricity became an outcome when somebody built the grid, set the standards, and formed the companies, cooperatives, and unions that made access stick. AI will become an outcome the same way, through firms, platforms, and economies built by people who understand that empowerment is not access alone. It is access, organized, under direction, with answerable parties inside the door.

So the predictions of AI ending the world mistake the location of the danger and the location of the remedy. The danger is not a valence the machine will assume on its own, because it can assume none absent the imbuement of a person’s will. The remedy is not to fear the power source or to plead with it, but to produce and organize the people whose wills will imbue it well. Kit’s encapsulation, affirmed in the exchange as the point of my work, can stand as the close: the power source is neutral; the outcome is a personnel question.

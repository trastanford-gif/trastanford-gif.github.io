The Math of Teacher Illiteracy

By Tra A.M. StanfordNovember 21, 2026

Most math teachers are illiterate.

The sentence will be misheard, so let it be heard properly first. It does not mean that math teachers cannot read. Most of them read perfectly well, write perfectly well, and calculate numerically better than almost anyone. If illiteracy meant only the inability to decode print, the sentence would be false, and misleading. It means something more exact, and the exactness is what makes it worth saying.

Literacy, stated formally, is the capacity to derive anything of fundamental need—stated formally again, it is the capacity to teach oneself. A literate person is not a person who has been stocked with what he needs. He is a person who can produce what he needs when the stock runs out: who can work a thing out from what he already holds, establish what he does not yet have, and so keep going without a supplier. Illiteracy, by the same definition, is not ignorance. A man may be full of information and still illiterate in this sense, if everything he has was handed to him and he can derive nothing on his own.

Professional literacy is that same capacity aimed at a job: “the capacity to assume and fulfill the duties of one’s job,” where duty is defined by the order of purpose, not the order of employment. For a teacher, the duty runs to the learner. So the question about a math teacher is not whether he is educated, credentialed, experienced, or sincere. It is whether he holds, in his own subject, that very capacity his job exists to build into someone else.

Now the subject. “Doing the math” is establishing mathematical objects, which is to say the concise definitions of conceptual objects in order that they become useful. “Using the math” is when calculations are being done with the aforementioned objects. The two are not the same activity, and the distance between them is where this essay lives. A teacher may be fluent in the second and never have performed the first. He can calculate with objects others established—demonstrate the procedures, assign the practice, grade the results fairly—and yet be unable to establish a single one of those objects himself, or say what the subject he teaches actually is.

Ask him. It is a simple question, and it has gone unanswered at scale for longer than anyone should be comfortable with: What is math? Almost any other teacher can define the subject that they teach. The history teacher points to the past; the biology teacher points to living things; their subjects exist in the world whether or not anyone defines them, and an honest answer is available even to a teacher who has never thought very hard. Math allows no pointing. Its objects are not found in the world waiting to be indicated; each one exists because it was concisely defined into usefulness. Which means that for the math teacher alone, defining the subject is not a preliminary to the work. It is the work—the doing itself. And it is the part his formation never required of him: not in his training, not in his certification, which tests only use, not in his hiring, not in the inspections and workshops that follow. At no point in the entire chain is the man asked to do the thing his subject is made of.

That is the math of it—the arithmetic by which the condition reproduces. Using-only instruction produces using-only graduates. Some of those graduates become teachers, and teach the only way they were ever taught: by handing over objects ready-made and training their use. Their students receive the same formation and call it math, and some of them, in turn, become teachers. At every step the instruments certify the wrong capacity and so detect nothing. The inability to do the math never appears on any record, because no record anywhere asks for it. What appears instead is fluency: correct answers, covered standards, passing scores—the visible products of math, in rooms where math itself was never done. Multiply the arrangement by a century and a profession, and the sum is what we have: a field that proclaims a subject it cannot define, staffed largely by people who were never given the chance to become literate in it. The public layer only makes the sum visible. There are hundreds of proclaimed math people online, amassing followings in the thousands, who could not define math to save their lives—and when the question is put to them plainly, in public, where any answer would do, there is no answer. Nearness is no rescue either. A teacher may be nearer to the real problem than most—may sense that answers are not the purpose, that reasoning matters—and still be unable to establish what the reasoning is reasoning about. Nearer than most is mathematically insufficient for the purpose of teaching, because the learner cannot spend a teacher’s proximity. Either the object is established for the child in front of him, or it is not.

None of this is a charge of bad character, and it matters that it not be read as one. The illiterate math teacher was himself once a learner in a room where the math remained undone. Nobody did the math with him, either. He is not the origin of the condition; he is its product and then its carrier, and the blame belongs where it always belongs in these matters: to the paid arrangement that formed him, certified him, and never once gave him the answer to what should be an inevitable question. He may be devoted. He may be beloved. Devotion is not literacy, and it does not convert into it.

The cost is paid by the only party who never agreed to any of it. The child in that classroom cannot distinguish between a teacher who cannot establish the subject and a subject that cannot be understood. Faced with procedures that operate on objects he was never given, and no sense anywhere to check them against, he draws the conclusion the arrangement has prepared for him: that math is a thing other minds are built for, and his is not. “And if math is not being done—only used—then no math is being taught.” If no math is being taught, then the child’s failure to learn it is not a failure at all. It is the one accurate measurement in the building.

What would literacy look like, in the teacher? Nothing exotic. A teacher who can say what math is, and stand behind the saying. Who can establish an object with a learner—make a fraction, a function, a proof into a thing the learner possesses—before asking the learner to use it. Who can derive what he needs when the script runs out, because his knowledge is not a stock of procedures but a capacity. Almost any other teacher is already asked to meet some version of this standard, because almost any other subject can be pointed to when the definition is demanded. Math, the subject whose objects exist only by being established, is the one subject that asks it of no one.

That most math teachers are professionally shy of a literacy that edifies the learner should not be internalized. A discipline based in concept—versus tangibility—calls for a personal discipline of self-interrogation. The teacher who puts the question to himself before he puts anything to his class—can I establish the object I am about to teach; do I know what it is, and what it is for—has already begun the remedy, because he has located the doing in the only place it can start. No instrument will ever ask it for him; the arrangement that formed him saw to that. But the question will never ask itself, and asking it does cost—in personal struggle, in the surrender of a self-image built on fluent use—and a man who learns to ask it of himself honestly, and pay, has taken the first step from using toward doing: toward the literacy that edifies the learner, and toward being able to answer, when the question finally comes from the other side of the desk, what math is. The willingness to pay that cost is, in fact, the first evidence of the literacy itself—a teacher who can interrogate his own practice is already deriving, already teaching himself. The struggle isn’t an obstacle to the remedy. It’s the remedy’s first installment.

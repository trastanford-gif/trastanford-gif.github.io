Why Tutoring Doesn’t Work

By Tra A.M. StanfordNovember 17, 2026

Tutoring is the respectable answer. A child is struggling in math, so the parent hires a tutor—someone patient, someone deemed qualified, an hour or two a week after school. It is the one remedy nobody questions. And it does not work. Not because tutors are incompetent, and not because the hours are too few, but because of what tutoring is. Its failure is structural, and no improvement in the quality of tutors will fix it, because the flaw is not in the tutoring. The flaw is in the definition of the job.

A tutor works inside someone else’s framework (or lack thereof) on the classroom’s terms—this week’s homework, Friday’s test. The terms of the work are set entirely by the classroom the child returns to every morning. And the parenthetic mention is the general case, and the general issue: in most classrooms there is no framework to work inside of. There is a curriculum—an arrangement of content over time—but no conception underneath it: no settled account of what the subject is, what it is for, or what counts as knowing it. That absence is the root problem, of which the classroom’s failure and the tutor’s failure are both instances. Tutoring did not create the absence, but it is built on top of it, takes its terms from it, and cannot rise above it. The tutor’s mandate is the student’s success on those terms—and those terms are the problem. If the class teaches rule-following without sense, then tutoring is more of the same teaching, delivered slower, kinder, and one-to-one. It cannot correct the conception, because its job is to make the student succeed within the conception. A tutor who set out to teach the child what math actually is, what it is for, and how it bears on the child’s life would be doing something else—something valuable, but no longer tutoring. The moment the work rises to the level where it could help, it has stopped being the thing that was hired for.

This is why the language of tutoring gives it away. Tutoring speaks of gaps—the student has gaps, the tutor closes them. But a gap exists only relative to an arrangement of content: a scope and sequence that says this piece should have been learned by October. The child’s real difficulty is rarely a missing piece. It is a missing conception—no working sense of what the subject is, from which the pieces could be strung together. Pieces do not compose into a conception by accumulation. A tutor can fill every gap on the list, in order, and leave the conception exactly as broken as it was, because the conception was never the assignment. The list was.

Consider the levels of the work. An instructor works at the level of the framework—what the subject is and what counts as knowing it. A teacher works at the level of the course. A tutor works at the level of the component, the lowest rung, the smallest unit of the arrangement. Tutoring is therefore the weakest possible intervention aimed at the strongest possible cause. One or two hours a week of component-level repair, set against twenty-five hours a week of the instruction-less curriculum that produced the damage—the whole weight of the school week flowing against the repair. The tutor is not failing to swim against the current. The tutor is employed by the current’s terms.

What tutoring reliably produces is not learning but performance. The student learns to produce what the teacher wants: the procedure executed, the homework completed, the test survived. Compliance rises, and because compliance is what the classroom grades, grades rise too. Then the rising grade does the system’s quiet work for it—it certifies the classroom method that caused the problem in the first place. The school points to the grade as evidence that its teaching works. The parent points to the grade as evidence that the money worked. And the child’s actual understanding, held separately from the performance all along, is never examined, because every number in sight says things are improving. Tutoring does not merely fail to expose the failure. It launders it into a success.

The economics complete the picture. Tutoring is commerce that profits from the failure it pretends to remedy, and its revenue depends on the failure recurring: the sessions are hourly, the need is renewable, and a student genuinely made literate is a customer lost. Meanwhile it absolves every paid party. The school can say the child needed extra help. The parent can say they did everything possible. And the blame lands, as it always lands, on the one unpaid party—now with a heavier verdict than before, because the child who still cannot do math even with a tutor must really be the problem. The system never has to change, because the child absorbs the failure, and tutoring is the mechanism that makes the absorption look like diligence.

None of this is an argument against help. It is an argument about the level at which help has to operate. Even the tutor has a legitimate form, and it is worth naming, because the name is the test. A tutor should be qualified by the framework he is a component-level practitioner in—examined and certified by it, scoped to its components, his standing held under the direction of whoever holds the framework whole. Where that is true, it shows in how he introduces himself: the framework’s name comes first—"I’m a Montessori tutor"—and the construction conveys certification in the framework, not mere familiarity with it. Where it is not true, there is nothing to put in front of the word. It should occur to every parent to wonder what framework a tutor is a tutor in, and to notice that the common answer—"I’m a math tutor"—names a subject and a market, but no framework, no certification, and no one the tutor answers to. The question costs nothing to ask. Most of the market cannot answer it.

A learner in difficulty does not need the components re-delivered at a slower pace inside the conception that failed him. He needs to be introduced—properly, from the beginning—to what the subject is, what it is for, and how it bears on his life, by someone whose work is governed by that conception and who answers for the learner’s literacy, not for Friday’s test. That work exists. It is called instruction. The tragedy of tutoring is that it occupies the place where instruction should be, spending the parent’s money and the child’s hours on the one form of help that is defined, from the first session, by its inability to reach the cause.
